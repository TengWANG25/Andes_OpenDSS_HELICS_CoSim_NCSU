\begin{abstract}
This paper proposes an open-source transmission-distribution (T\&D) co-simulation framework for the analysis of fault-induced delayed voltage recovery (FIDVR). The framework couples an IEEE 14-bus transmission system modeled in ANDES with an IEEE 13-node distribution feeder modeled in OpenDSS, while HELICS coordinates the exchange of interface voltage and feeder complex power. To represent residential air-conditioner response, selected single-phase feeder loads are decomposed into a residual static component and a WECC Motor D-type compressor-motor component. The proposed framework is used to study the effect of motor share, feeder voltage diversity, transmission fault location, and post-fault restoration stages. Simulation results show that the Motor D share mainly affects the post-clearing reactive-power demand rather than the fault-period voltage nadir. The results also show that the feeder-head voltage can recover while downstream nodes remain depressed, and that transmission faults away from the interface bus can still trigger feeder-level delayed recovery. A 45~s case study further illustrates the sequence of fault dip, delayed recovery, capacitor insertion, restartable motor return, regulator tap action, and thermal load restoration.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
fault induced delayed voltage recovery, transmission-distribution co-simulation, dynamic load modeling, HELICS, OpenDSS, ANDES, composite load model.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}

Transmission and distribution systems are often simulated with different modeling assumptions. Transmission models are commonly represented as balanced positive-sequence dynamic networks, whereas distribution feeders are radial, unbalanced, and include device-level voltage controls. This separation is computationally convenient, but it can hide relevant interactions between bulk-system disturbances and feeder-level dynamics \cite{inproceedings,article}. Fault-induced delayed voltage recovery (FIDVR) is one example in which this interaction is essential. FIDVR is characterized by a sustained low-voltage profile following the clearing of a transmission fault, and several such events have been reported in North American systems \cite{207331,miller2014wws_phase3}.

A FIDVR event is initiated by a fault. After the fault is cleared, the voltage first recovers only partially and can remain depressed for several seconds. The North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) describes FIDVR as a condition involving induction motor stalling, incomplete initial voltage recovery, and delayed restoration of the voltage toward its pre-contingency level \cite{nerc2025dynamicloadmodeling,lesieutre2012loadmodeling}. 
\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{report_assets/fidvr_signature.png}
    \caption{Representative FIDVR voltage recovery trajectory \cite{article7}.}
    \label{fig:fidvr_signature}
\end{figure}
Figure~\ref{fig:fidvr_signature} illustrates the typical voltage trajectory
of a FIDVR event.
The dominant physical mechanism is the stalling of single-phase induction motors in residential air conditioners. During a severe voltage sag, stalled compressor motors draw a large reactive current. This current further depresses the local voltage, delays motor recovery, and can lead to contactor dropout, thermal tripping, or additional motor stalling. The resulting feedback between load behavior and voltage recovery is the main reason why FIDVR cannot always be represented accurately by a static load model.

The objective of this work is to evaluate FIDVR using a co-simulation formulation that preserves transmission dynamics and feeder-level voltage resolution. The main contributions are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item an ANDES-OpenDSS-HELICS workflow for dynamic T\&D FIDVR studies;
    \item a feeder-level Motor D implementation that separates selected single-phase loads into static and compressor-motor components;
    \item a set of case studies that quantify motor-share sensitivity, feeder voltage diversity, transmission fault-location effects, and the sequence of post-fault restoration events.
\end{enumerate}
Section~II reviews motor-level FIDVR models, aggregate composite load models, and T\&D co-simulation methods. Section~III presents the modeling framework and assessment metrics. Section~IV presents the validation and case-study results. Section~V summarizes the main conclusions and future work.

\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{FIDVR Mechanism and Load Modeling}

Residential air-conditioning load is a major driver of FIDVR. A U.S. Department of Energy survey reported that a large share of households use either central or room air-conditioning equipment; consequently, faults in areas with high air-conditioner penetration can lead to widespread motor stalling \cite{6863324}. Therefore, an adequate representation of single-phase A/C motors is essential for reproducing the delayed voltage-recovery process.

Several studies have modeled the compressor motor explicitly. Ravikumar \emph{et al.} developed an EMTP-based point-on-wave model of single-phase air-conditioner motors and tested the response under different voltage-depression conditions \cite{inproceedings1}. Zheng and DeMarco proposed a dynamic performance model that represents the transition from running to stalled operation and the subsequent thermal trip process \cite{7444204}. These studies show that FIDVR is not determined only by fault duration or voltage magnitude. It is also affected by motor loading, protection settings, contactor behavior, and thermal trajectories.

A second approach is to represent the phenomenon through aggregate dynamic load models. The WECC composite load model includes a dedicated single-phase air-conditioner motor component and associated protection logic \cite{bravo2009weccacmotor}. The NERC dynamic load modeling reference provides practical default parameters for the single-phase motor component commonly referred to as Motor D \cite{nerc2025dynamicloadmodeling}. The Motor D model provides a compact
performance-based representation of compressor-motor behavior during voltage
sags. In the original formulation\cite{bravo2009weccacmotor}, a prescribed fraction of the
constant power load is reassigned to an equivalent air-conditioner motor, while
the remaining part of the load is represented by the associated static load
model. This representation retains the main FIDVR mechanisms of residential
compressor motors, including voltage-dependent stalling, stalled-state active
and reactive power consumption, contactor action, thermal protection, and
partial restart. It is computationally simpler than an EMT motor model and is
therefore suitable for feeder-level dynamic studies within a T\&D
co-simulation framework. Its limitation is that the motor is represented by a
performance model rather than by instantaneous electromagnetic equations.
Consequently, point-on-wave effects, dc offsets, and detailed flux dynamics are
not resolved. These effects require EMT modeling, but at a substantially
higher computational cost and with a smaller practical study area
\cite{bravo2009weccacmotor,ravikumar2016emtp,huang2016hybridfidvr}.

Composite load models and EMT models are based on different modeling
assumptions and are therefore suited to different studies. The composite load
model is an aggregate phasor-domain model. It is computationally efficient and
well suited to large-scale dynamic simulations, but the single-phase compressor
motor is described through an equivalent performance model rather than through
instantaneous electromagnetic variables. Conversely, an EMT model resolves the
point-on-wave evolution of voltages, currents, flux linkages, and torque. It can
therefore represent phase-angle dependence, dc-offset components, and
unbalanced waveform effects that are not retained in a positive-sequence
formulation. This increased physical resolution, however, is obtained at the
expense of a substantially higher computational cost and a reduced practical
study area \cite{ravikumar2016emtp,huang2016hybridfidvr}.

\subsection{Transmission-Distribution Co-Simulation}

T\&D co-simulation keeps the transmission and distribution models in separate simulation tools while coordinating time advancement and exchanging boundary variables through middleware. In a typical dynamic T\&D interface, the transmission simulator sends the interface-bus voltage to the distribution simulator, and the distribution simulator returns the complex feeder power to the transmission simulator. This structure allows each subsystem to retain its native models and numerical solution methods while appearing externally as a coupled system.\cite{9569771}

Huang \emph{et al.} proposed an open-source framework for power-system T\&D dynamic co-simulation based on the Framework for Network Co-Simulation (FNCS) \cite{article1}. Palmintier \emph{et al.} later described the HELICS architecture, which was developed to coordinate large-scale co-simulation studies involving power, communication, market, and end-use simulators \cite{inproceedings2}. HELICS also supports iterative time advancement. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:HELICS}, the outer loop advances the global simulation time, whereas the inner loop exchanges boundary variables among federates at a fixed time instant. If the boundary mismatch is above the convergence tolerance, the federates remain in the \texttt{ITERATING} state. Once the boundary variables are consistent, HELICS grants the next time step and the co-simulation proceeds.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{report_assets/helics_candidate_2.jpg}
    \caption{Iterative time-advancement structure of the HELICS-based co-simulation.}
    \label{fig:HELICS}
\end{figure}

Different tool combinations have been used for T\&D co-simulation. Early steady-state and quasi-static studies coupled MATLAB with OpenDSS for IEEE 9-bus or IEEE 39-bus transmission systems and EPRI distribution feeders \cite{8805231,article3}. Other studies used MATLAB-based transmission and distribution models for coordinated T\&D optimal power flow \cite{article4}. Integrated grid studies coupled FESTIV or MATPOWER with GridLAB-D to connect bulk-system operation with distribution and end-use models \cite{7558187}. For dynamic studies, researchers have used PSCAD/EMTDC with InterPSS for hybrid EMT-phasor simulation, mixed three-sequence and three-phase models for unbalanced T\&D dynamics, Three Phase Dynamics Analyzer for combined T\&D dynamic simulation, and GridLAB-D or EMT/OpenDSS for distribution-side dynamic studies \cite{8356599,7782366,7426417,7024951,7582536}. Federated dynamic co-simulation has also been studied using FNCS with dynamic three-phase models and GridLAB-D, PSAT with OpenDSS for integration time step analysis, and PSLF with OpenDSS for distributed generation response to bulk system faults \cite{article1,8485735}.

More recently, Wang \emph{et al.} developed an open-source ANDES-OpenDSS-HELICS framework for DER frequency-response studies \cite{9569771}. In that framework, ANDES represents the transmission dynamics, OpenDSS solves the distribution network, and HELICS coordinates the exchange of boundary quantities. The framework is particularly relevant because it demonstrates the feasibility of an open-source and scalable T\&D dynamic co-simulation architecture. However, its main objective is DER frequency response rather than FIDVR. The present work uses the same general software philosophy but focuses on feeder-level compressor-motor stalling and delayed voltage recovery.

\section{Modeling Framework}

\subsection{General Dynamic Representation}

The transmission system is represented by the following set of differential-algebraic equations (DAEs) \cite{1490569}:
\begin{align}
    \dot{x}_T &= f_T(x_T,y_T,u_T,p_T), \\
    0 &= g_T(x_T,y_T,u_T,p_T),
\end{align}
where $x_T$ denotes the transmission dynamic states, $y_T$ is the algebraic variables, $u_T$ is the boundary input received from the distribution feeder, and $p_T$ is the model parameters.

For feeder $k$, the distribution model is written as
\begin{align}
    \dot{x}_{D,k} &= f_{D,k}(x_{D,k},y_{D,k},u_{D,k},p_{D,k}), \\
    0 &= g_{D,k}(x_{D,k},y_{D,k},u_{D,k},p_{D,k}).
\end{align}
For a quasi-static OpenDSS feeder, the differential states may be absent from the network solution itself. In this study, motor stalling, contactor dropout, thermal tripping, and restart logic are represented as discrete dynamic states in the Python layer surrounding OpenDSS.

At the interface bus, the transmission simulator sends the feeder-source voltage and the distribution simulator returns the aggregate feeder power. The exchanged variables can be written as
\begin{align}
    u_{D,k} &= V_{abc,k}, \\
    u_T &= \sum_{k=1}^{N_f} S_{abc,k},
\end{align}
where $V_{abc,k}$ is the three-phase interface voltage supplied to feeder $k$, $S_{abc,k}$ is the three-phase complex power drawn by feeder $k$, and $N_f$ is the number of coupled feeders.

The proposed assessment uses the IEEE 14-bus transmission system coupled to the IEEE 13-node feeder at bus 14. ANDES solves the transmission dynamic model, OpenDSS solves the distribution feeder, and HELICS coordinates the boundary-variable exchange. The transmission federate publishes the interface-bus voltage, denoted by \texttt{TxInterfaceVoltage}. The distribution federate publishes the aggregate feeder complex power, denoted by \texttt{Feeder\_Power}. The overall structure is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:cosim}.

\subsection{Motor D Parameterization}

The compressor-motor representation follows the WECC single-phase air-conditioner Motor D structure \cite{bravo2009weccacmotor}. For each selected phase load $i$, the original active power $P_{0,i}$ is split as
\begin{align}
    P_{m,i} &= F_m P_{0,i}, \\
    P_{s,i} &= (1-F_m)P_{0,i},
\end{align}
where $P_{m,i}$ is the motor component, $P_{s,i}$ is the remaining static component, and $F_m$ is the motor fraction. The initial motor reactive power is computed from the compressor power factor as
\begin{equation}
    Q_{m,i}=P_{m,i}\tan\left[\cos^{-1}(\mathrm{CompPF})\right].
\end{equation}
For the default value $\mathrm{CompPF}=0.97$, the initial reactive-to-active power ratio is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{Q_{m,i}}{P_{m,i}}=0.251.
\end{equation}
Thus, before stalling, the motor component has a moderate reactive-power demand.

During the stalled condition, the Motor D implementation uses the stall impedance parameters. The equivalent conductance and susceptance are
\begin{align}
    G_{stall} &= \frac{R_{stall}}{R_{stall}^2+X_{stall}^2}, \\
    B_{stall} &= \frac{X_{stall}}{R_{stall}^2+X_{stall}^2},
\end{align}
and the corresponding stalled active and reactive powers are
\begin{align}
    P_{stall} &= G_{stall}V^2, \\
    Q_{stall} &= B_{stall}V^2.
\end{align}
Using $R_{stall}=0.124$, $X_{stall}=0.114$, and $V_{stall}=0.70$, the stalled load factors at the stall threshold are approximately
\begin{equation}
    P_{stall}\approx2.14P_m, \qquad Q_{stall}\approx1.97P_m.
\end{equation}
The stalled motors therefore impose a large reactive power demand on the feeder and can delay the restoration of the post-fault voltage. The default parameters used in the implemented feeder model are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:motor_d_params}.

\subsection{FIDVR Assessment Metrics}

The assessment compares two main cases:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Baseline co-simulation case:} the IEEE 13-node feeder is coupled to the transmission system without the added single-phase compressor-motor groups.
    \item \textbf{FIDVR-enabled case:} the compressor-motor groups are added with different motor fractions to evaluate the sensitivity of post-fault recovery to motor penetration.
\end{enumerate}

The following quantities are used to evaluate the response:
\begin{align}
    V_{min}^{T} &= \min_t |V_T(t)|, \\
    V_{min}^{fh} &= \min_t |V_{fh}(t)|, \\
    V_{min}^{load} &= \min_{\ell,t} |V_{\ell}(t)|, \\
    Q_{max}^{D} &= \max_t Q_D(t), \\
    T_{0.90}^{D} &= \min\{t>t_c: |V_D(t)| \geq 0.90\}, \\
    T_{0.95}^{D} &= \min\{t>t_c: |V_D(t)| \geq 0.95\}, \\
    N_{stall}(t) &= \text{number of stalled motor groups}.
\end{align}
Here, $V_T$ is the interface bus voltage, $V_{fh}$ is the feeder-head voltage, $V_{load}$ is the voltage of monitored load bus $\ell$, $Q_D$ is the feeder reactive power seen by the transmission system, and $t_c$ is the fault clearing time. The recovery times $T_{0.90}^{D}$ and $T_{0.95}^{D}$ directly measure the delayed voltage recovery process.

The comparison is performed between two T\&D co-simulation cases. The baseline
case uses the original feeder loads without added compressor motors. The
FIDVR-enabled case uses the same system and disturbance, but selected
single-phase loads are split into a residual static component and a WECC compressor-motor component. Therefore, the
difference between the two cases isolates the effect of compressor-motor
stalling.

The motor-induced voltage depression is defined as
\begin{align}
    \Delta V_{\min}^{fh} &=
    V_{\min}^{fh,\mathrm{base}} - V_{\min}^{fh,\mathrm{motor}}, \\
    \Delta V_{\min}^{load} &=
    V_{\min}^{load,\mathrm{base}} - V_{\min}^{load,\mathrm{motor}}.
\end{align}
Positive values indicate that the compressor-motor component deepens the
post-fault voltage sag. The corresponding recovery-time delay is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \Delta T_{\alpha} =
    T_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{motor}} - T_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{base}},
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
    T_{\alpha}^{c} =
    \min \{t>t_c: |V_{load}^{c}(t)| \geq \alpha \},
    \qquad
    c\in\{\mathrm{base},\mathrm{motor}\}.
\end{equation}
Here, $t_c$ is the fault clearing time and $\alpha$ is the selected recovery
threshold, such as $0.90$ or $0.95$ p.u. A larger $\Delta T_{\alpha}$ indicates
that compressor-motor stalling delays the post-fault voltage recovery. The
increase in feeder reactive power demand is also quantified as
\begin{equation}
    \Delta Q_{\max}^{D} =
    Q_{\max}^{D,\mathrm{motor}} - Q_{\max}^{D,\mathrm{base}}.
\end{equation}

\section{Case Studies}

This section evaluates the proposed framework through four complementary studies. The first verifies the co-simulation workflow against an established large-scale T\&D benchmark. The second quantifies the sensitivity of post-fault recovery to the Motor D share. The third illustrates the feeder-level voltage diversity that is hidden by a single equivalent load. The fourth evaluates whether transmission disturbances away from the interface bus can still lead to feeder-level delayed voltage recovery. Unless otherwise stated, the monitored distribution signal is the average voltage magnitude at node 675, the transmission interface bus is bus 14, the fault begins at $t=1.0$~s and clears at $t=1.08$~s, and the Motor D share is applied to the selected single-phase loads listed in Table~\ref{tab:feeder_info}.

\subsection{Validation Case: IEEE 118-Bus and IEEE 8500-Node System}

The ANDES-OpenDSS-HELICS workflow is first checked using an IEEE 118-bus transmission system coupled to an IEEE 8500-node feeder. This case is compared with an existing GridPACK-GridLAB-D-HELICS implementation based on the same boundary-variable exchange. The purpose of this step is not to calibrate the FIDVR model, but to verify that the transmission voltage trajectory, aggregate feeder power, and HELICS synchronization are consistent with an established T\&D co-simulation workflow. The smaller IEEE 14-bus and IEEE 13-node system is then used for the remaining studies because it allows the feeder response, motor-state changes, and transmission fault-location effects to be interpreted explicitly.

\subsection{Motor-Share Sensitivity}

The 0\% case is used as the benchmark because all selected loads retain their original static OpenDSS representation. Hence, the delayed recovery is not mainly a consequence of a deeper fault-period voltage sag; it is caused by the elevated post-fault reactive-power demand of stalled and recovering compressor motors.

\subsection{Feeder-Level Voltage Diversity}

The second case uses the same bus-14 fault and 100\% Motor D share, but monitors several IEEE 13-node feeder locations. This case evaluates the information lost when the feeder is replaced by a single equivalent load. Figure~\ref{fig:node_diversity} compares the average voltage at the feeder nodes. Node 650 is the upstream feeder-head node, whereas node 652 is a downstream node. Although the fault produces a deep voltage sag throughout the feeder, the post-clearing recovery is spatially non-uniform. At 10~s, node 650 recovers to approximately 0.995~p.u., whereas node 652 remains near 0.813~p.u. and node 675 remains near 0.892~p.u.

A single equivalent distribution load connected at the interface bus can reproduce the aggregate feeder power, but it cannot identify which downstream nodes experience the deepest or longest voltage depression. Feeder-level voltage resolution is therefore necessary for locating the nodes most exposed to FIDVR.

\subsection{FIDVR Alert and Load-Restoration Case}

The final case extends the high motor-share simulation to 45~s to capture the post-fault restoration sequence. Figure~\ref{fig:stage_view} shows the node-675 voltage trajectory and the main event markers. The fault produces a fast voltage dip at approximately 1.01~s. The feeder voltage then remains below the normalized 0.90~p.u. delayed-recovery threshold for 5~s, and the delayed-recovery alert is triggered at 6.01~s. Capacitors are fully inserted at approximately 6.09~s. The 20\% restartable motor component completes its short restart path at approximately 15.49~s, and the regulator tap reaches its maximum at approximately 18.09~s. The voltage later enters an overvoltage stage and triggers the third alert at 28.14~s.

The 45~s simulation also clarifies why a 30~s simulation is not sufficient to observe the load-restoration effect. Thermal load restoration begins at approximately 28.23~s, close to the 30~s endpoint. By 45~s, the thermal restoration fraction has increased to approximately 0.29, node 675 remains near 1.05~p.u., and the Motor D reactive-power scale has decreased from its post-fault peak while remaining above the pre-fault level. The late recovery is therefore a staged interaction among motor restart, thermal tripping, capacitor insertion, regulator tap boost, and restored load.

\section{Conclusion}
\balance

This paper has proposed and evaluated a feeder-level FIDVR study workflow based on ANDES, OpenDSS, and HELICS. The framework preserves the transmission dynamic model while resolving the distribution feeder and its Motor D compressor-motor response.

The following conclusions can be drawn. First, the Motor D share is a dominant sensitivity parameter. Increasing the Motor D share does not substantially change the fault-period voltage nadir, but it increases the post-fault reactive-power demand and delays voltage recovery. Second, feeder-level voltage diversity is significant. The feeder head can recover close to nominal voltage while downstream nodes remain depressed, which cannot be observed with a single equivalent-load representation. Third, transmission faults away from the interface bus can still affect the distribution feeder. Faults at buses 13, 7, and 4 propagated through the interface voltage and produced delayed recovery at node 675, although their severity differed from the direct bus-14 fault. Fourth, the transmission and distribution alert responses are different. Transmission bus 14 triggered only the fast fault-dip alert, whereas distribution node 675 triggered the fault-dip, delayed-recovery, and recovery-overshoot alerts.

These results indicate that FIDVR is a coupled T\&D phenomenon whose critical recovery stages can occur inside the feeder after the bulk interface voltage has apparently recovered. Future work should extend the study to multiple feeders and larger transmission networks, calibrate the Motor D, capacitor, and regulator parameters against utility or benchmark FIDVR data, and evaluate mitigation strategies such as motor protection settings, feeder capacitor control, regulator coordination, and dynamic reactive-power support.
